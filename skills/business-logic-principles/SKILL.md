# PRINCIPLES — The reasoning behind the rules

If `SKILL.md` is *what* and `VOICE.md` is *how*, this file is *why*. Use this when something in the other files seems to conflict with a new situation — the principle below should tell you which side to come down on.

---

## 1. Who buys, and what that means

### Scandinavian buyer psychology

Norway and Sweden are small, high-trust markets shaped by **Janteloven** — a cultural reflex against standing above the group. American-style hype reads as desperate, untrustworthy, and slightly embarrassing. The buyer's default position is skepticism, and the seller's job is to earn the right to be heard.

This is why:
- **Specific beats vague.** A specific number, a named person, a concrete moment — these signal that the seller actually knows something. Vague claims signal the opposite.
- **Content-driven beats urgency-driven.** Urgency without substance reads as manipulation. Substance without urgency still converts; urgency without substance does not.
- **Real names beat anonymous proof.** Anonymous testimonials read as fabricated in a market this small.
- **No superlatives.** "The best," "world-class," "revolutionary" — these don't scale up trust, they scale it down.

When in doubt, ask: *would a sharp, skeptical Norwegian friend roll their eyes at this?* If yes, rewrite.

### Individual decision-makers, not buying committees

Most AweSM offers (kr 19,950 and similar) sit in a price band where **the buyer is the decision-maker** — the person clicking "buy" is also the person paying and the person using the product. This is fundamentally different from B2B SaaS or enterprise sales.

What this means:
- **No "get your manager's approval" logic.** That logic destroys conversion when the buyer is sovereign — it implants doubt and delegates the decision to someone who won't make it.
- **No HR/L&D budget framing.** It positions the offer as employer-paid when it's actually individual-paid, which collapses urgency and shifts the psychological weight to the wrong place.
- **Decision kits help with self-justification, not committee approval.** The post-webinar kit is for the buyer to convince themselves over coffee the next morning, not to sell their boss.

This is the lesson from the **Krystallklart Budskap** funnel reversal — the entire architecture flipped from committee logic to individual logic, and that's the standard now.

### When B2B logic *is* right

For offers genuinely sold into companies (a 30–50 employee SMB hiring AweSM as an agency, for example), buying-committee logic returns. The test isn't price — it's **who signs the invoice** and **who feels the consequence**.

## 2. Why webinar funnels

A webinar is the highest-leverage format for selling **belief-dependent offers** at a price point too high for impulse and too low for sales calls. The buyer needs to:

1. **Trust the teacher** (that's the teaching segment),
2. **Believe a new thing is possible for them** (that's the big idea / unique mechanism),
3. **See the path** (that's the transformation case study),
4. **Decide to act now** (that's the structured pitch + offer).

Most webinars fail at #2 and #4. They teach competently, then either skip the belief-shift or do it generically, then collapse into an awkward pitch transition that makes the whole thing feel like a bait-and-switch.

The AweSM framework rebuilds in this order:
- **Story-driven open** → earn attention.
- **Belief-shift / unique mechanism** → make the offer make sense before it appears.
- **Transformation case studies** → name a real person, show before/after, anchor the mechanism.
- **Bridge to pitch** → explicit, structured, not apologetic. The pitch is the payoff for showing up.
- **Structured offer** → stack, price, urgency (real, not invented), CTA.

**Why we never turn off chat during pitch:** chat is social proof during the highest-stakes moment of the funnel. Active chat = "other real people are here, and they want this." Silence kills it. The recommendation to turn it off is a comfort move (less heckling, less distraction) that costs more conversion than it saves.

## 3. Two funnel architectures

| | Direct Sale Funnel | Application Funnel |
|---|---|---|
| Price | 4,000–15,000 NOK | 20,000–80,000 NOK |
| Decision speed | Same session, or short follow-up | Days to weeks |
| Sales mechanism | Self-serve checkout | Booked call, qualification |
| Risk for buyer | Low — refundable, low total cost | High — material commitment |
| Copy job | Convince to buy now | Convince to apply / book |

These aren't preferences — they're determined by the offer. A 4k NOK product **cannot** sustain a sales-call funnel (CAC math collapses). A 60k NOK program **cannot** be sold by checkout alone (the buyer needs human reassurance before that level of commitment).

Mixing the architectures is one of the most common mistakes — running an application funnel for a 5k product (too much friction) or self-serve checkout for a 50k program (too little trust). The price tells you which architecture, not the other way around.

## 4. Why content-driven, not urgency-driven

Urgency works. But urgency without substance trains the buyer to wait for the next discount. **Content-driven funnels build the asset** — a list of people who trust the brand and have learned something — that then makes urgency *credible* when used.

Sequence:
1. Content earns the relationship.
2. Substance makes the offer credible.
3. *Then* a real deadline (price increase, cohort cap, bonus expiration) creates urgency that the buyer respects rather than resents.

Urgency without #1 and #2 is just pressure. Pressure converts in the short term and burns the list in the long term. In a market the size of Norway, you only get to burn the list once.

## 5. Why Wildr Japan reads differently

Wildr is a **premium, trust-dependent product**. The buyer is committing real money, real time, and real physical risk (backcountry skiing, avalanche terrain). Push-style booking CTAs would sabotage the positioning.

What works for Wildr:
- **Insider details** — names of bowls, snow conditions, local spots — signal that the operator actually knows Hokkaido. Generic "epic powder" copy signals the opposite.
- **Conversation-first CTAs.** "Send us a message" lets the lead self-vet and lets us vet them. The conversation is the qualification.
- **Sensory writing.** This is a sensory product. Copy that doesn't put the reader in the boots, on the slope, in the snow, isn't doing its job.
- **Small groups as the differentiator.** Not the cheapest, not the most extreme — the most personal.

The Wildr buyer is making a 5–15k EUR decision. They want to feel like they're being invited into something specific, not closed by a funnel.

## 6. Why this team structure

AweSM runs lean (≈10 people) and location-independent because:
- **Webinar funnels are repeatable IP.** The framework doesn't need 50 people to deploy — it needs a small team that knows it cold.
- **High-ticket margin absorbs lean ops.** When average client revenue is in the tens of thousands of NOK, you don't need scale, you need quality.
- **AI automation replaces process labor.** The Auto AI system exists so the team doesn't grow linearly with client count.

Implication for any system Claude helps build: **prefer leverage over scale.** A system that lets one team member do the work of three is worth more than a system that lets us hire two more.

## 7. Why specificity is the universal rule

Across copy, code, internal docs, and Claude's own responses, the same principle applies: **specificity is the cheapest signal of competence**. A vague answer can be bluffed; a specific one cannot.

This is why I push back on:
- "Dette," "det," "vi viser deg hvordan" without antecedents — vague reference is vague thinking.
- Anonymous testimonials — undifferentiated proof.
- "Many students get results" — could be three, could be three hundred.
- "Improves conversion" — by how much, on which page, for which traffic source?

When Claude drafts something for me and it reads generic, the fix is almost always: **add a specific name, number, place, moment, or detail** until the sentence couldn't have been written by someone who didn't know the situation firsthand.
